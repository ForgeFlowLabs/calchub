How Many Calories Do I Need Per Day?

Your daily calorie needs depend on your age, sex, body weight, height, and how active you are. This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation — the most widely validated formula for estimating Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) — then multiplies by your activity level to get your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE). Your goal adjustment is applied on top to give your daily calorie target.

The Mifflin-St Jeor Formula

Men: BMR = (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) + 5
Women: BMR = (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Your TDEE is then calculated as: TDEE = BMR × Activity Multiplier, where the multiplier ranges from 1.2 (sedentary) to 1.9 (extremely active).

Understanding Macros

Macronutrients — protein, carbohydrates, and fat — are the three sources of dietary calories. Protein and carbs each provide 4 calories per gram; fat provides 9 calories per gram. This calculator uses a balanced default split of 30% protein / 40% carbs / 30% fat, which works well for most goals. Higher-protein splits (35–40%) are often recommended for fat loss to preserve muscle mass.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE? ▾
BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) is the number of calories your body needs at complete rest — just to keep your heart beating, lungs breathing, and organs functioning. It accounts for roughly 60–75% of total daily calorie burn. TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) adds the calories burned through daily activity and exercise on top of BMR. TDEE is the number you use for nutrition planning — it's your true maintenance calorie level.
How fast should I lose weight? ▾
A deficit of 500 calories/day creates roughly 1 lb of fat loss per week (since 1 lb of fat ≈ 3,500 calories). This is widely considered a safe, sustainable rate. Deficits larger than 750–1,000 calories/day increase the risk of muscle loss, nutrient deficiencies, fatigue, and rebound weight gain. The most important factor for long-term success is choosing a deficit small enough to maintain without feeling deprived.
Why is my calorie estimate different from other calculators? ▾
Different calculators use different equations. The three most common are Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict (revised), and Katch-McArdle (which requires body fat %). Research consistently shows Mifflin-St Jeor is the most accurate for the general population. However, all equations are estimates — metabolic rate can vary by 10–15% between individuals with the same stats due to genetics, hormones, and muscle mass.
How much protein should I eat per day? ▾
General guidelines suggest 0.8g of protein per kg of body weight for sedentary adults. For active individuals and those trying to lose fat while preserving muscle, 1.6–2.2g per kg (0.7–1g per lb) is more optimal. Higher protein intake increases satiety, preserves lean mass during a caloric deficit, and has a higher thermic effect (your body burns more calories digesting protein than carbs or fat).
Does calorie counting actually work? ▾
Yes — at the fundamental level, weight change is driven by the calorie balance equation (calories in vs. calories out). However, calorie labels on food can be off by up to 20%, portion estimation is difficult, and appetite hormones can make rigid counting unsustainable for many people. Most nutrition researchers recommend using calorie awareness rather than obsessive tracking — understanding roughly what maintenance looks like and adjusting based on weekly weight trends.
Why do calories matter less as you lose weight? ▾
As your body weight decreases, your BMR decreases — you need fewer calories to maintain your new smaller body. This is why weight loss typically slows over time even with the same diet. It's not a "broken metabolism" — it's a natural adaptation. Recalculate your TDEE every 10–15 lbs of weight change to keep your targets accurate. Increasing activity level is often more sustainable than further reducing calories.
