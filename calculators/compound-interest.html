What Is Compound Interest?

Compound interest means earning interest on both your original principal and the interest already accumulated. Unlike simple interest (which only earns on the principal), compound interest creates exponential growth — your interest earns interest. Over decades, this effect is dramatic: $10,000 at 7% for 30 years grows to over $76,000 with no contributions, purely from compounding.

The Compound Interest Formula

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

A = final amount, P = principal, r = annual interest rate (decimal), n = compounding periods per year, t = time in years. With monthly contributions added, the formula becomes a sum of compounded future values for each contribution.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does starting age affect compounding? ▾
Dramatically. Starting at 25 vs. 35 with $500/month at 7% means roughly $600,000 more at age 65 ($1.37M vs. $0.73M). The first decade of contributions does most of the heavy lifting because those dollars compound the longest. This is why "start early" is the single most important piece of investment advice — time is a more powerful variable than rate or contribution amount.
Does compounding frequency matter much? ▾
Surprisingly little. On a $10,000 investment at 7% for 20 years: annual compounding yields ~$38,697; monthly compounding yields ~$40,387 — a difference of just $1,690. Daily vs. monthly is even smaller. What matters far more is the rate itself, how long you invest, and how consistently you contribute. Don't obsess over compounding frequency.
What is a realistic rate of return to use? ▾
The S&P 500 has returned ~10% annually before inflation and ~7% after inflation over long periods (30+ years). High-yield savings accounts in 2025 offer 4–5%. Treasury bonds average 3–5%. For long-term retirement planning, financial planners typically use 6–7% as a conservative real-return assumption. Never use past returns as a guarantee of future performance.
What is the Rule of 72? ▾
The Rule of 72 is a mental math shortcut: divide 72 by your annual interest rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 6%, doubling takes ~12 years (72 ÷ 6). At 10%, it takes ~7.2 years. The rule is accurate within 1–2% for rates between 2% and 15%. It also works in reverse: to double your money in 8 years, you need roughly a 9% return (72 ÷ 8).
How does inflation affect my investment returns? ▾
Inflation erodes purchasing power. A 7% nominal return with 3% inflation gives a ~4% real return. Enter your expected inflation rate in this calculator to see the inflation-adjusted (real) value of your investment — what that future balance would be worth in today's dollars. For long-term planning, always think in real returns rather than nominal ones.
What's the best account type for compound interest? ▾
Tax-advantaged accounts maximize compounding because you're not losing a portion to taxes each year. A 401(k) or Traditional IRA defers taxes until withdrawal. A Roth IRA grows tax-free — all the compounded growth is yours. The difference between taxable and tax-free growth is substantial over decades: on a $100,000 account growing at 7% for 30 years, tax-free growth yields ~$761,000 vs. ~$510,000 at a 25% annual tax drag.
