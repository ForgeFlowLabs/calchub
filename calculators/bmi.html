What Is BMI?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a simple calculation using your height and weight to estimate whether you're at a healthy weight for your height. It was developed by Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet in the 1830s and adopted by the World Health Organization as a population-level screening tool. It remains the most widely used weight classification system in clinical and public health settings.

The BMI Formula

BMI uses different formulas depending on the unit system:

Metric:    BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²)
Imperial: BMI = 703 × weight (lbs) ÷ height² (in²)

For example, a person who is 5'10" (70 inches) and weighs 175 lbs: BMI = 703 × 175 ÷ 70² = 703 × 175 ÷ 4900 ≈ 25.1 (just into the overweight range).

BMI Categories (WHO Standard)

The World Health Organization defines four main BMI categories for adults: Underweight (below 18.5), Normal weight (18.5–24.9), Overweight (25.0–29.9), and Obese (30.0 and above). Obesity is further divided into Class I (30–34.9), Class II (35–39.9), and Class III / severe obesity (40+).

Frequently Asked Questions

Is BMI an accurate measure of health? ▾
BMI is a useful population-level screening tool but has real limitations at the individual level. It cannot distinguish between muscle mass and fat mass — a highly muscular athlete may have a BMI in the "overweight" range despite having very low body fat. It also doesn't account for where fat is distributed (visceral fat around organs is more dangerous than subcutaneous fat). For a more complete picture, healthcare providers use BMI alongside waist circumference, body fat percentage, blood markers, and clinical judgment.
Does BMI differ by age? ▾
The standard BMI categories apply to adults 20 and older. For children and teenagers (2–19 years), BMI is interpreted differently using age- and sex-specific percentiles from CDC growth charts, not fixed cutoffs. For older adults (65+), some research suggests that a slightly higher BMI (22–27) may be protective, as low body weight in the elderly is associated with frailty and increased mortality.
Does BMI differ by ethnicity? ▾
Yes. The standard BMI cutoffs were developed primarily from studies on White European populations. Research shows that people of Asian descent tend to have higher body fat percentages at lower BMI values. The WHO has recommended lower action points for Asian populations: overweight at BMI ≥23 and obese at BMI ≥27.5. Some health organizations also note that Black individuals may have higher muscle mass and bone density, meaning standard BMI cutoffs may overestimate health risk.
What's the difference between BMI and body fat percentage? ▾
BMI is a simple ratio of weight to height squared and can be calculated with just a scale and tape measure. Body fat percentage is the actual proportion of your body that is fat tissue versus lean mass (muscle, bone, organs, water). Body fat percentage is more informative but harder to measure accurately — it requires methods like DEXA scan, hydrostatic weighing, or a Navy tape measurement. What is a healthy BMI for women vs men? ▾
The official WHO BMI categories (18.5–24.9 for normal weight) are the same for both men and women. However, at the same BMI, women typically have a higher body fat percentage than men due to physiological differences in fat distribution. A BMI of 22 in a woman might mean ~28% body fat, while the same BMI in a man might mean ~18% body fat. This is why sex-specific body fat percentage assessments can be more informative than BMI alone.
How can I lower my BMI? ▾
Since BMI is based on weight and height (and height doesn't change), lowering BMI means losing weight — specifically body fat. A sustainable approach combines a moderate caloric deficit (typically 500 calories/day below maintenance, which creates roughly 1 lb/week of fat loss), regular aerobic exercise, and resistance training to preserve muscle mass. Crash diets can lower BMI quickly but often result in muscle loss and quick weight regain. Use our Calorie Calculator to find your maintenance calories and plan a deficit.
