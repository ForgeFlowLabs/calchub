What Is ROI?

Return on Investment (ROI) measures the efficiency of an investment — how much you earned relative to how much you put in. It's expressed as a percentage and works for any type of investment: stocks, real estate, business projects, marketing campaigns, or education.

ROI vs CAGR — Which Should You Use?

ROI = (Final Value − Initial Cost) / Initial Cost × 100%
CAGR = (Final / Initial)^(1 / years) − 1

ROI tells you the total return over the entire period. CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) annualizes that return, making it useful for comparing investments held for different periods. A 50% ROI over 10 years is actually a mediocre 4.1% CAGR — CAGR reveals this where simple ROI does not.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good ROI? ▾
It depends on the investment type and timeframe. For stocks, a 7–10% annual return (CAGR) is historically average. For real estate, 8–12% total return (including appreciation and rental income) is typical. For business projects, many companies use a hurdle rate of 15–25% ROI to approve spending. Marketing ROI above 5:1 ($5 return per $1 spent) is generally considered good.
What is the difference between ROI and CAGR? ▾
ROI is the total return over the holding period with no time adjustment. CAGR is the annualized rate — the constant yearly growth rate that would produce the same total return. CAGR is better for comparing investments over different time periods. Example: Investment A returns 100% in 3 years (CAGR: 26%). Investment B returns 100% in 10 years (CAGR: 7.2%). Same ROI, very different annual performance.
What is payback period? ▾
Payback period is the time needed to recoup your initial investment. If you invest $10,000 and earn $3,333/year, the payback period is 3 years. It's a simple risk measure — shorter payback means lower exposure. However, it ignores time value of money and returns beyond the payback point, which is why it should be used alongside ROI and CAGR, not instead of them.
How do fees affect ROI? ▾
Fees have an outsized impact on long-term ROI because they compound against you. A 1% annual fee on a $100,000 portfolio over 30 years at 7% growth costs over $180,000 in lost growth — more than the original investment. This is why low-cost index funds have systematically outperformed actively managed funds after fees over long periods. Always calculate net-of-fees ROI for any investment.
