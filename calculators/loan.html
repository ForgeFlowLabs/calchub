How to Use This Loan Calculator

Select your loan type to load typical rate and term defaults, then adjust the fields to match your actual loan offer. Enter an optional extra monthly payment to see how much interest you'd save and how many months sooner you'd pay off the loan. The amortization schedule shows the exact breakdown of every payment.

The Loan Payment Formula

All fixed-rate loan payments use the same amortization formula:

M = P × [r(1+r)ⁿ] / [(1+r)ⁿ − 1]

P = principal (loan amount), r = monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12), n = total number of monthly payments. For example: a $25,000 loan at 7.5% over 60 months gives r = 0.625%, n = 60, M ≈ $501/month.

What Is an Origination Fee?

An origination fee is a one-time charge by the lender to process your loan, typically 1–8% of the loan amount. It's either deducted from your loan proceeds or added to the balance. A $25,000 loan with a 3% origination fee means you pay $750 upfront but still owe $25,000. This calculator shows the effective APR when a fee is included — which is the true cost of the loan and how lenders are required to disclose it.

Why Extra Payments Matter So Much

Every extra dollar you pay goes directly to principal — not interest. This reduces the balance on which future interest accrues, creating a compounding benefit. On a $25,000 loan at 7.5% over 60 months, paying just $50 extra per month saves over $350 in interest and pays the loan off 3 months early. The higher the interest rate, the bigger the impact.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good interest rate for a personal loan in 2025? ▾
Personal loan rates in 2025 range from about 6% to 36% APR depending on credit score and lender. Rates under 10% are excellent and typically available to borrowers with credit scores above 720. Rates of 10–15% are average. Anything above 20% is expensive and worth shopping around to beat. Credit unions and online lenders often offer better rates than traditional banks.
What's the difference between APR and interest rate? ▾
The interest rate is the base cost of borrowing. APR (Annual Percentage Rate) includes the interest rate plus any fees — like origination fees — expressed as a yearly rate. APR gives you the true cost of the loan. When comparing loan offers, always compare APRs, not just interest rates. A loan with a lower rate but high fees can be more expensive than one with a slightly higher rate and no fees.
Should I choose a shorter or longer loan term? ▾
Shorter terms have higher monthly payments but significantly lower total interest. Longer terms ease monthly cash flow but cost much more overall. Use the term comparison table above to see exactly how much you'd save. As a rule of thumb: if you can comfortably afford the higher payment, the shorter term almost always wins financially.
Does paying off a loan early hurt my credit? ▾
Paying off a loan early can temporarily lower your credit score by a small amount because it removes an active account from your credit mix. However, the financial savings from eliminated interest almost always outweigh this minor score impact. Check your loan agreement for prepayment penalties before paying early — some lenders charge a fee for early payoff.
What credit score do I need for a loan? ▾
Most personal loan lenders require a minimum credit score of 580–600, but you'll need 670+ to get competitive rates. At 720+ you'll qualify for the best rates. If your score is below 600, consider a secured loan, credit union, or improving your score before applying. Each hard inquiry from a loan application can temporarily lower your score by a few points.
How is an auto loan different from a personal loan? ▾
Auto loans are secured by the vehicle — if you don't pay, the lender can repossess the car. Because they're secured, auto loan rates are typically lower than unsecured personal loans. Auto loan terms are usually 24–84 months. Longer auto loan terms (72–84 months) significantly increase total interest and can leave you "underwater" — owing more than the car is worth.
What is debt-to-income ratio and why does it matter? ▾
Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is your total monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income. Lenders use this to assess how much more debt you can handle. A DTI below 36% is generally considered healthy. Above 43% and many lenders will decline your application or charge a higher rate. To calculate yours: add up all monthly debt payments and divide by your gross monthly income.
